Justworks Enterprise Design HR Tech · PLG

EOR Cost
Calculator

I was Justworks' first designer in Toronto. My first project cut international cost reporting from 1 hour to 5 seconds — and became the foundation for the company's PLG self-enrollment strategy.

Justworks is an HR platform that simplifies payroll, benefits, and compliance for thousands of small businesses. When the company pushed into international markets, I joined as its first product designer in the Toronto office — hired specifically to shape how global customers would experience Justworks for the first time. My first project: an Employer of Record (EOR) Cost Calculator that could tell a business owner exactly what it costs to hire someone in Germany, Canada, or Mexico — in five seconds.

Role
Senior Product Designer
Company
Justworks
Timeline
2024 – 2025
Team
PLG · International Expansion
The Problem

Sales reps were spending an hour on every cost estimate — and losing deals because of it

Before the calculator existed, generating a single international cost report was entirely manual. A sales rep would spend up to an hour per request: sourcing tax rates from government websites, fixing broken Excel formula references, and emailing a PDF that was sometimes outdated by the time the client opened it.

The business impact was significant — slow sales cycles, inconsistent outputs that eroded trust, and prospects who moved on before getting an answer. Competitors like Oyster, Deel, Remote, and Omnipresent already had self-serve calculators. Justworks didn't. This wasn't just a workflow problem. It was a conversion problem.

The Decision That Shaped Everything

Before I touched Figma, I made the strategic case for why this needed to be a product — not a spreadsheet

The obvious brief was "make a better calculator." I didn't start there. Instead I spent two weeks interviewing the sales team and building the business case for why this tool needed to be treated as a strategic product asset — tied directly to Justworks' FY25 international revenue goals and competitive positioning.

I presented to stakeholders before designing a single screen, connecting the tool to three business levers: lead conversion (prospects who got faster estimates were significantly more likely to move forward), sales efficiency (eliminating the manual quoting burden), and competitive differentiation (matching and beating what Deel and Oyster already offered). That framing changed the scope and ambition of the project.

"Both internal and external users valued speed and trust above all else."
Research & Discovery

The research revealed two completely different users calling themselves the same thing

I interviewed sales reps, prospects, and internal operations teams — mapping their workflows with emotional markers to capture not just procedural friction, but the anxiety underneath it. One insight changed the architecture of the entire design.

Not all cost estimate requests were the same. Exploratory clients were pre-offer: they needed fast, rough estimates to decide if international hiring was financially viable at all. Confirmed-offer clients were post-offer: they needed precise, finance-team-ready numbers before a deal could close. These two users had opposite needs — one wanted speed, the other wanted accuracy and depth. Designing one tool to serve both, without bloating it, required an explicit architectural decision: adaptive fields that changed based on country selection, a clear scope disclaimer, and a report format that finance teams could trust.

Exploratory clients (pre-offer) needed fast rough estimates — they were still deciding if international hiring was feasible
Confirmed-offer clients (post-offer) needed precise numbers for their finance team before a deal could close
Quoting complexity varied drastically by country: Canada and Mexico were simple; France and Germany were expensive with high compliance overhead
No standardized output meant every deliverable looked different — clients compared quotes from different reps and got confused
Design Process

Two-stage workshops, adaptive inputs, and an API-first architecture

01
Two-stage ideation workshops
I ran a small session first — designers and PM only — to generate ideas freely without engineering constraints. Then a second, larger workshop with Engineering, Sales, and Finance using an Impact × Effort matrix to prioritize scope. This sequencing was intentional: the first session lets ideas breathe, the second grounds them in reality. The MVP feature set came directly from that second workshop.
02
Competitive audit of existing calculators
Before wireframing, I audited Oyster, Deel, Remote, and Omnipresent's cost calculators to identify patterns, gaps, and where we could differentiate. The insight: competitors gave estimates but rarely explained them. Our opportunity was in the explanation — the transparency layer on top of the numbers.
03
Wireframes and adaptive field logic
Low-fidelity wireframes validated the core layout decisions: adaptive inputs that changed based on country selection (reducing cognitive load for simpler markets like Canada while surfacing additional complexity for France and Germany), the employer vs. employee cost split view, and fallback states for incomplete or unknown data. Engineering flagged technical constraints before they became blockers.
04
API-first, responsive high-fidelity
The final design was built API-first — not just a UI skin over a spreadsheet, but a live data-connected tool. Responsive layouts for desktop, breakpoint, and mobile. The report output was designed to be finance-team-shareable, with a clear employer/employee cost breakdown and an intentional disclaimer: estimates are for planning, not payroll decisions or legal agreements.
05
Design system documentation
Components and tokens from this project were documented and added to the Justworks design system. Recorded walkthroughs were produced for engineering handoff — improving design-to-code velocity for the PLG team on subsequent features.
Results

5 seconds. 700 hours saved per month. The calculator became the foundation for PLG.

The calculator delivered immediate, accurate employment cost estimates by country — covering salary, taxes, and benefits — accessible to both sales reps and prospects through self-service. First-session activation increased as more users reached a trusted price before dropping off. The design was later extended into Justworks' self-enrollment flow, allowing customers to initiate the onboarding journey directly from their quote.

Whole Foods Market Enterprise Design Retail Operations

Supplier & Offer
Data Management

Part of Whole Foods' Centralized Data Management Platform — I mapped 12+ legacy systems before designing anything, and that decision determined whether the new platform would succeed or recreate the same fragmentation in a cleaner wrapper.

Whole Foods Market (an Amazon subsidiary) managed supplier and item data across more than a dozen disconnected legacy systems — IRMA, SNIP, ICon NIF, IX-One, FoodLogIQ, and more. I was brought in as Senior Product Designer to lead UX for the supplier and offer data management workflows within a larger Centralized Data Management Platform initiative. My focus: design the system that let teams and suppliers set up, edit, approve, and track item offers — consolidating six separate tools and five intersecting user roles into one trusted interface.

Role
Senior Product Designer
Company
Whole Foods Market (Amazon)
Timeline
2023 – 2024
Scope
Supplier & Offer Data Workflows
The Problem

The same data lived in 12 different places. Everyone had a different version of the truth.

Before this initiative, setting up a single new product offer at Whole Foods required a supplier to send forms to a Merchant, who transferred a NIF form into a NISSS submission, which was reviewed by the MIM team, checked by GDO, SCP, and DC review teams — through a combination of email, SharePoint, Excel, Teams, and proprietary forms with names that required a glossary to understand.

Each department — merchandising, category management, finance, supply chain — had its own entry point and its own version of the data. The result: redundant work, inconsistent pricing, strained supplier relationships, and difficulty tracing where errors had entered the system. No one owned the whole picture. That was the real problem.

The Decision That Shaped Everything

I spent the first weeks auditing every legacy system before designing anything

The temptation was to jump straight to wireframes. Instead, I made a deliberate call to spend the first weeks of the project auditing every legacy tool — cataloging every data field, every downstream dependency, every team that touched each piece of data. I mapped the current-state flow in full before any design work began.

This felt slow. It was the right decision. The audit revealed the root cause: this wasn't a UI problem, it was a data ownership problem. The new platform couldn't just be a cleaner interface — it needed to be a governance system, with role-based views, multi-step approval workflows, and an audit trail that made every data change traceable and accountable. A prettier wrapper on the same broken ownership model would have failed within months.

"The audit revealed that no single team owned the full data picture — and designing around that, rather than past it, was the only approach that would hold."
Design Process

Five design sprints, two strategic calls, and one cross-squad initiative I initiated myself

01
Legacy system audit & current-state mapping
Catalogued all data fields, dependencies, and user roles across IRMA, SNIP, ICon NIF, IX-One, FoodLogIQ, and the internal spreadsheet ecosystem. Facilitated journey mapping workshops to visualize cross-team handoffs and identify where data broke down. This became the foundation for all IA decisions.
02
The Meridian Design System decision
Working as an Amazon subsidiary, I made a deliberate call to adopt Amazon's Meridian Design System rather than building from scratch. This wasn't just an efficiency decision — Whole Foods' platform needed to eventually integrate with Amazon Retail data infrastructure, and Meridian created the architectural groundwork for that. I ran workshops with both WFM and Amazon designers to make it work while preserving Whole Foods' brand identity.
03
Cross-squad workshop initiative
Recognizing that the platform touched multiple squads with overlapping responsibilities, I initiated several rounds of cross-squad design workshops — something that wasn't standard practice. The goal was to identify UX opportunities, establish shared patterns, and build a culture of consistency across what would become a complex, multi-team product. This is where the error taxonomy was defined before a single error state was designed.
04
Five design sprints with usability testing at each stage
Offer Detail Page → Edit Single Item → Edit Multiple Items → Offer Setup Workflows → Bulk Offer Edit. Each sprint followed the same rhythm: design mocks, test with users, hand final files to dev. Weekly stakeholder check-ins kept engineering, PM, and business stakeholders aligned throughout.
05
Error taxonomy & audit trail design
Before designing any error states, I defined an error taxonomy — distinguishing warnings, validation errors, and system alerts. This systems-level decision is what produced the 60% reduction in late-stage validation errors. The audit trail component (showing user, timestamp, and field-level changes) was designed for regulatory compliance, not just usability.
Results

$4M+ saved annually. 200,800+ labor hours returned. One source of truth.

Centralizing supplier and offer data replaced over a dozen legacy tools with a single governed interface — giving all five user roles a trusted, traceable view for the first time. Proactive error handling caught issues upstream rather than at submission, and the new design system slashed the time needed to ship subsequent features.

Ledn Product Design Fintech · Crypto

B2X Lending
Platform

On my first week at Ledn, I opened a real B2X loan and watched the Bitcoin market for a week. That decision shaped every design choice I made after it.

Ledn is a digital asset platform — like a bank, but for crypto. Bitcoin savings accounts, USDC yields, Bitcoin-backed loans. The B2X (Bitcoin × 2) product let users borrow against their Bitcoin to buy more Bitcoin, doubling their market exposure. It was Ledn's most profitable product. But the existing interface was a dense modal of unstyled numbers: collateral value, LTV percentage, outstanding balance — displayed without hierarchy, context, or any emotional scaffolding for people making a financially significant decision.

Role
Product Designer
Company
Ledn
Timeline
2021 – 2022
Product
B2X Loan · Dashboard · KYC
The Problem

The original platform gave users numbers. It didn't give them understanding.

The existing B2X interface showed loan details in an unstyled modal: two tabs of raw data — collateral required, daily interest, loan amount, administration fee, annual rate, expiry date. Everything was there. Nothing was explained. For a product where a 10% Bitcoin price drop could trigger a margin call and liquidate a user's collateral, "here are the numbers" wasn't enough.

Users couldn't track how collateral value moved with the market. They didn't understand what their LTV ratio meant in practice. Margin call warnings existed but were easy to miss. The result: high drop-off in loan applications, high support volume from confused users, and low repeat borrowing — despite strong market conditions.

The Decision That Shaped Everything

On my first week, I put real money into a B2X loan and watched the market for a week

I joined Ledn knowing nothing about crypto lending. Rather than reading documentation, I opened a real B2X account and invested my own money. For one week I watched the Bitcoin price move. I felt the excitement when it climbed and the anxiety when it dropped. I experienced exactly what our users experienced — not through a walkthrough, but through genuine financial exposure.

That week gave me something no brief could: a felt understanding of the emotional states the design needed to address. I knew from the inside that the anxiety didn't come from seeing the numbers — it came from not understanding what they meant. That became the design principle that shaped everything: transparency over simplicity. Don't hide the complexity. Explain it.

"Users didn't want complexity hidden from them — they wanted it explained. The anxiety came not from seeing the numbers but from not knowing what to do with them."
Research & Discovery

20+ interviews confirmed what I'd already felt firsthand

I ran more than 20 rounds of user interviews and usability testing sessions with retail crypto investors. The findings matched my own experience: users wanted to understand their collateral health at a glance, see exactly when a margin call would be triggered, and know what actions were available to them — without needing to calculate anything themselves.

I also audited competitor lending platforms in both crypto and traditional finance, and interviewed internal stakeholders across Customer Success and Compliance. The compliance team's constraint shaped the design as much as user research did: every risk disclosure had to be accurate and prominent, not buried in tooltips or fine print.

Users understood their loan balance but didn't understand their LTV ratio or what a "healthy" LTV looked like
Margin call warnings existed but were easy to miss — users often discovered them after the fact
First-time borrowers needed more guidance; repeat borrowers needed faster access to management actions
Compliance required risk disclosures to be prominent and plain-language — not hidden in a tooltip
Design Process

IA first, then two-stage workshops, then design

01
Current-state IA before any wireframes
Before sketching a single screen, I built a full information architecture map of the existing B2X product — every screen, every state, every decision point. Then I built a future-state IA incorporating the new features. This gave Engineering a clear structural picture before any wireframes existed, eliminating a whole category of late-stage surprises and rework.
02
Two-stage ideation workshops
I ran a small ideation session first — designers and PM only — to generate ideas freely without engineering constraints. Then a second, larger cross-functional workshop with Engineers and Customer Success using an Impact × Effort matrix to prioritize scope. The MVP feature set came directly from that second session. The sequencing matters: the first session lets ideas breathe, the second grounds them in reality.
03
The transparency-first design decisions
LTV as a dynamic visual gauge rather than a static percentage — so users could read collateral health at a glance. Proactive margin call alerts shown before liquidation events, not after. Plain-language summaries sitting beside financial terminology throughout the loan flow. Collateral health indicators that visibly moved with the Bitcoin price.
04
Prototype testing & iteration
Interactive prototypes were tested with first-time retail borrowers and experienced users. I watched where they hesitated, what they ignored, and which moments triggered questions. Each cycle tightened the flow. By the final round, participants could explain their LTV status and next steps without prompting — the specific behavioral signal we'd set as our success criterion.
05
Cross-functional compliance alignment
Partnered with Engineering to wire live BTC pricing feeds and risk models into the UI. Legal and Compliance reviewed every disclosure and tooltip — clarity couldn't compromise regulatory accuracy. Ran go-live checklists with Support and Ops so alerts, notifications, and handoffs behaved as intended on day one.
Results

Support tickets dropped 92%. B2X became Ledn's most-used product during the 2021 bull market.

Making the loan experience transparent and self-explanatory removed the need for users to reach out for help. Customer support requests dropped from 5 emails per day to 2 per week — a 92% reduction. New loan openings increased and repeat borrowing grew. B2X became Ledn's most-used lending product during the 2021 bull cycle. KYC completion rates improved by 25%.

Lemon · Futureproof Labs Growth Design macOS · AI-Native

Onboarding &
Monetization

I changed the roadmap before I changed the design — pulled retention data after a viral launch and argued we needed to fix the leaky bucket before adding features.

Lemon is a macOS productivity app that lives in the menu bar notch and uses AI to capture, transcribe, and act on what you say and see — turning interruptions into decisions. After a viral Product Hunt launch, I joined as Lead Product Designer. The metrics told a different story than the excitement: less than 30% of users came back after 48 hours. Before I touched Figma, I changed the roadmap.

Role
Lead Product Designer
Company
Futureproof Labs / Lemon
Timeline
April – May 2026
Focus
Onboarding · Monetization · Retention
Act 1 — The Strategic Call

The launch went viral. I pumped the brakes.

Feature requests were piling in, the roadmap was filling up, and the team's instinct was to build faster. I looked at a different number: less than 30% of new users came back in the first 48 hours. We were filling a leaky bucket.

I went deeper — segmented retention by acquisition channel, identified the percentage of users who had made zero API calls after completing onboarding, and researched how Wispr Flow (our closest competitor) had reached 20% free-to-paid conversion. They didn't get there by shipping features. They got there by obsessing over whether users retained the core value first.

"Even if we make the best product in the universe — if we can't keep new users coming back, it's worthless. Retention before features."

I synthesized 103 Slack messages across 9 team channels into a product insights deck, used it to make the data-backed argument, and the roadmap changed. From that point on, I shipped a new onboarding version every week with controlled groups, measuring what actually moved retention.

Act 2 — The Onboarding Solution

Give before get — and let the medium teach

The diagnosis was clear: the original flow was "get before give." It asked for permissions, role, and preferences before showing users anything of value. The fix wasn't visual — it was a philosophy: show users what Lemon does for them before asking for anything. When they feel the value first, granting permissions feels like unlocking something they want, not a gate they're being pushed through.

Five weeks of A/B tests on split-panel designs (V1–V6) gave incremental wins. Then on April 29 came the real insight: Lemon lives in the Mac notch. A full-screen onboarding window was already breaking the product's promise before the user had started. I abandoned five weeks of work and designed the entire 23-screen flow to live inside the notch, floating over the user's real desktop. The UI container became the teacher.

V5 was the best conventional approach — full-screen, clean, polished — but the wrong medium for a product that promises "no new windows"
V8 insight: the notch expands on the actual desktop, introducing itself exactly as it will always live — invisible, always there
Screen 12: when screen recording permission is requested, the wallpaper itself turns golden-yellow — Lemon demonstrates the permission instead of explaining it
Screen 19 "Speed Receipt": "You just saved 43 minutes" appears immediately after the aha moment, with the trip plan still visible behind it
Act 3 — The Monetization System

The team wanted a cold email. I built an empathy system instead.

The original plan: send an email to all existing users announcing the free trial was ending and they'd need to pay. No lead-up, no context, no empathy. I pushed back hard — researching how Wispr, Raycast, Superhuman, and Notion handled conversion, and making the case that paywalls that feel like punishment kill conversion while paywalls that feel like opportunity drive it.

The team heard it. They pushed back the trial end date. And I designed the upgrade experience from scratch — with no prior monetization layer existing in the product at all.

"We don't want to give users surprises like this. Monetization is a sensitive moment — it needs to be treated with the most empathy and transparency."

Rather than a single paywall, I designed 16 distinct upgrade moments organized into four categories: Lifecycle (transparent about time), Gates (usage limits), Value (what Lemon did for you), and Relationship (habit and recognition). Every single one leads with celebration or empathy before the ask.

Act 4 — Design System

The "give before get" philosophy only works if it's consistent everywhere

Alongside the onboarding and monetization work, I audited the entire production design system — finding 15 missing components, wrong font references (Solway instead of Inter), and green colors that violated the brand. The resulting specification document, including dark mode tokens and a complete component library, ensures the empathy-first philosophy is consistent across every screen, not just the ones I personally touched.

All 23 onboarding screens were designed using the Figma MCP directly on canvas. I built a custom Claude Code skill for multi-direction ideation, proposing 7 new screens (Speed Receipt, Notch Dictionary, Taste Profile) and building all approved ones in a single session. Weekly prototype delivery included HTML animations for each use case sequence.

🎁
Give before get
Show value before asking for permissions, data, money, or personalization. Applied consistently from screen 1 of onboarding to the final upgrade modal.
📊
Data drives direction
V8 was triggered by a drop-off node in the analytics. The retention argument was built on segmented channel data. Surveys measured willingness to pay at peak intent moments.
🔦
The medium is the message
The notch IS the product. Onboarding lives where the product lives. Form and function teach the same thing — so the product's promise is never broken before the user has started.
💬
Empathy is a strategy
Acknowledging user pain before pitching. Celebrating what users built before asking them to pay. This isn't softness — it's the mechanism that makes conversion work.
Results

8 tested versions. A full monetization system. A changed roadmap.

In five weeks: 8 onboarding iterations shipped with A/B test infrastructure, 16 upgrade moments designed across 4 categories, the entire upgrade system built from zero, and a design system audit that brought consistency across all product surfaces. The strategic call to prioritize retention over features created the foundation the product needed before growth would compound.
